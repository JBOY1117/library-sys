Beyond the Ember Peaks

"The hardest journey is not the one your boots make, but the one your conscience does."

✍️ Jerome Geraldo
~1,600 words
⏱ ~8 min read
🏔️

The Ember Peaks earned their name from what happened there at dusk. The last light of the sun caught the iron-red stone of the ridgeline and set it burning — orange, then gold, then a searing crimson that looked, from the valley below, like a crown of fire balanced along the spine of the mountains. Travellers who had never seen them gasped. Travellers who had seen them before looked away.

Kael had been looking at them for three weeks. Long enough for the spectacle to become familiar, then ominous, then something he refused to name.

He was twenty-four years old, half-starved, and carrying a satchel that contained a water skin, a dented compass, two strips of cured venison, and a sealed letter that had been given to him by a dying cartographer in the city of Velm with strict instructions: Cross the Peaks. Find the Hollow below the third summit. Leave this inside. Do not open it. Do not read it. Do not speak of its contents, because you will not know them, and that is the only mercy I can give you.

He had agreed. He had been paid half upfront. He needed the money more than he needed explanations.

The Crossing

The climb began before dawn on the twenty-second day. The path — if you could call it a path; it was more a series of suggestions made by goat hooves across crumbling scree — wound upward through darkness so complete that Kael navigated by the stars alone, using the compass less for direction than for the reassurance of something solid in his palm.

By midday he had passed the treeline. The air here was thin and cold, carrying the smell of stone and altitude — a clean, inhuman smell that reminded him there were places in the world that did not concern themselves with human presence whatsoever. He found this thought oddly comforting.

He ate the second strip of venison sitting on a flat ledge with his feet dangling over a two-hundred-foot drop, watching a hawk work the thermal below him. The hawk was hunting. He admired its calm efficiency. Below the hawk, far below, the patchwork of farmland and forest that had taken him three weeks to cross looked like a cloth someone had dropped carelessly on the floor.

"The world is larger than your purpose in it," his mother used to say, when he came home from the docks disappointed at a day's poor wages. He used to find this unhelpful. Up here, he finally understood what she meant.

The summit of the first peak came in the late afternoon. He did not stop to admire it. The second peak followed an hour later, the path there threading across a narrow ridge that required all four limbs and all of his concentration. Once, his right foot slipped and he hung for three heartbeats from his hands, fingers jammed into a crack in the rock, the world tilting vertiginously around him, and he thought very clearly: I am going to die here for a letter I haven't read.

Then he hauled himself up and kept moving.

The Hollow

The third summit sat inside a saddle of rock that sheltered it from the worst of the wind. Below it, exactly where the cartographer's rough sketch had indicated, was the Hollow: a natural bowl in the stone perhaps thirty feet across, its floor sheeted in a frost that had never melted, so old it had become like glass. At the centre of the bowl was an object that stopped Kael dead at the rim.

A tree. Growing from bare, frozen stone, without soil, without water, without any visible reason for existing. It was old — older than anything Kael had ever seen alive. Its bark was silver-grey, its branches leafless and reaching upward like supplicating hands. At the base of its trunk, like letters carved by someone working slowly over years, were names.

Hundreds of them.

He descended into the Hollow carefully, the frost cracking under his boots like thin ice over a pond. He crouched at the base of the tree and read names at random. Some were in languages he recognised. Others were scripts he had never seen. All of them felt completed — old in the way a locked room is old, carrying the specific gravity of finished things.

He looked at the letter in his hand.

He understood in that moment what the cartographer had meant by mercy. He did not know what was in the letter. He did not know whose name he was delivering, or what act of commission or absolution the delivery represented. He was a courier, ignorant and clean.

He could remain so. That was the choice.

The Weight of the Unread

Kael sat in the Hollow for a long time. The sun dropped below the western ridgeline and the Ember Peaks did their famous burning above him, but down here the colour was muted, softer — the light of something reaching rather than blazing. He could hear nothing. No wind, no bird, no sound from the world below. The Hollow held a silence that felt deliberate, like the silence of someone who is listening.

He thought about the cartographer — old Rennik, who had pressed the letter into his hands with fingers already cold, whose eyes had carried something Kael had read as urgency but now reconsidered as grief. Not the grief of a man dying. The grief of a man who had made a choice.

He thought about the names in the bark. They had all been delivered by someone. Carried up here by couriers or by the people themselves, each one a weight set down at the foot of an impossible tree at the edge of an impossible world.

He rose. He pressed the sealed letter against the bark between two carved names, and he felt — he could not explain it, could not have explained it to anyone — he felt the letter taken. Not snatched. Taken gently, the way you take something from a sleeping child's hand so that they don't drop it. The paper was there and then it was not, and in its place there was a name carved shallow and new in the grey bark, already beginning to weather at the edges, as though time here moved in its own way.

He did not look at the name.

The mercy was in the not-looking. Rennik had given him that, at least.

The Descent

Kael climbed out of the Hollow as the last light left the sky. The descent was faster than the climb — it always was — and he moved through the blue-dark with a sureness in his feet that he had not had on the way up, as if the mountain was done with him and was willing to let him go. By midnight he had passed below the treeline again. By dawn he was in the foothills, and by the following evening he could see the lights of the first village below.

He never told anyone what he had found in the Hollow. Not for secrecy, but because he lacked the vocabulary. Some things refuse to be carried in language; they must be carried in the body, in the particular weight of a choice made correctly in a high, cold, silent place.

He spent the cartographer's second payment on a meal and a bed and a week's worth of rest. Then he packed his satchel and moved on, because that was the nature of the work, and because moving was the only way he knew to keep the Hollow from becoming a place he visited only in dreams.

But he always, after that, moved lighter. As if something had been received on his behalf as well — something old and carried too long, set at the feet of an impossible tree, taken gently, without ceremony.

As if the mountain had known what he needed before he did, and had the patience to wait until he arrived.
