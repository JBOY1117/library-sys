Blood and Chrome

"One hour. One floor. One way out — and it's already been compromised."

✍️ Jerome Geraldo
📖 ~1,580 words
⏱ ~8 min read
📅 2026
🔫

The earpiece crackled and Dara Voss heard the voice of her handler, Wen, say three words: "Clock is running." She had been standing in the maintenance stairwell of the Vanek Tower for eleven seconds. She had fifty-nine minutes and forty-nine seconds left before the building went into total lockdown — not a corporate drill, not a fire alarm, but the kind of silence that a building performs when someone at the top decides that what is inside it should stay inside forever.

She had been paid to make sure it didn't.

"Floor twenty-two," she said quietly, pulling the compact from her jacket and checking her face out of habit — not vanity, but to confirm nothing on her exterior would flag her to the cameras in the ceiling. She looked like a building manager. Conservative jacket. Laminate badge courtesy of Wen's forgery contact. Hair up. Comfortable shoes, because uncomfortable ones got you killed in corridors when you needed to be quiet and fast simultaneously.

She took the stairs.

The Ascent

The Vanek Tower was forty-three floors of corporate architecture that had stopped pretending to be beautiful somewhere around floors fifteen through twenty-five, where the glass and chrome interior gave way to raw concrete and service conduits. This was where they kept what they didn't want visitors to see. Dara had studied the schematics for two weeks. She knew every angle of every camera, every patrol route, every door that required a biometric scan versus one that required only a swipe card.

Floor seventeen. A guard was posted at the landing — young, bored, running his thumb over his phone screen with the distracted propriety of someone who had decided nothing would happen tonight. Dara came through the door with her clipboard at chest height, already speaking.

"HVAC fault on eighteen, I need to check the relay junction—" She was past him before he finished processing her badge, one hand already moving to the door release. Confidence, Wen always said, is not about what you feel. It's about what the other person decides to believe.

Floor eighteen. The relay junction was real; she had tripped it herself from the basement forty minutes ago. The service panel was open, a technician crouched over it. She nodded at him with the curt familiarity of people who share a building but not a job, and kept walking.

"Speed is not about moving fast," her instructor at the advanced programme had told her class, making them walk a timed assault course at a controlled pace. "Speed is about never stopping. The moment you pause to think is the moment someone else finishes thinking for you."

Floor twenty-two. She paused at the door. Forty-one minutes left.

The Target

His name was Soto — Dr. Emilio Soto, former lead research analyst for the Vanek Corporation's third-tier pharmaceutical division, current involuntary resident of a room labelled on the official building manifest as "Server Management Suite 4." He had not been seen outside of that room in six weeks. The people who had put him there were not worried about his comfort. They were worried about what was in his head — specifically, the fourteen months of research he had conducted before someone in accounting noticed an anomaly in the trial data and realised what he had found.

Dara didn't know the specifics. She had learned, over years of this work, not to collect the specifics. The specifics had a way of following you home.

The door to Server Suite 4 required a palm scan. She pressed her left hand flat against the scanner and held her breath. Wen had promised the spoof would work. The scanner's indicator light held red for four seconds — the longest four seconds of Dara's recent career — and then turned green. The lock disengaged with a sound like a held breath releasing.

Soto was sitting on the floor beside the server rack, back against the humming metal casing, knees drawn up. He was in his early fifties, unshaven, wearing the same clothes he'd presumably been delivered in. When the door opened he looked up with the precise expression of a man who has calculated his situation so many times that he has stopped being surprised by its elements.

"You're not from the building," he said.

"No," said Dara. "Can you walk?"

"Yes."

"Then we're leaving."

The Descent

They had been in the stairwell for ninety seconds when the alarm began. Not the HVAC fault she had engineered — the real alarm, the grey and toneless klaxon that told every security team in the building that something valuable was moving through it without permission. Dara had expected thirty-two minutes before discovery. This was twenty-three.

"Someone in the building is watching cameras in real time," she said into the earpiece.

"I see it," Wen said. "Floor fifteen, they're repositioning. Floor nine, two on the east stair. You need the west shaft."

The west maintenance shaft was a steel-runged ladder drop that descended from floor fourteen to the basement sub-level. It was not a comfortable route. Dara looked at Soto, who was already breathing harder than she liked after only three floors.

"I need to ask you something," she said.

"Now?" He was incredulous.

"How bad are you with heights?"

He stared at her. "Reasonably bad."

"Okay. Don't look down."

The shaft was cold and smelled of grease and old metal. Dara went first, moving hand-over-hand down the rungs with Soto following three feet above, his breathing controlled with visible effort, his eyes — she could tell by the sound of his movement — fixed on the back of her head rather than the drop below. The klaxon was muffled by the shaft walls but not eliminated. Somewhere above them she heard a door bang open.

"Faster," she said, and he moved faster, and she admired him for it.

Ground Level

The sub-basement smelled of diesel and concrete dust. The building's underground service road connected to an access gate on the east side of the block — the only exit not yet covered, according to Wen, whose voice was now coming in broken fragments through increasing interference. Dara had three minutes by her estimate before that changed.

She and Soto moved through the sub-basement at a pace that was not quite a run because running attracted attention even in empty rooms, by some law of physics she had never been able to articulate. She kept one hand on his arm, guiding him through the turns she had memorised, and he followed without protest.

The service gate was padlocked with a chain. She had a bolt cutter in the lining of her jacket. It took twelve seconds. The gate swung outward into an alley, where a grey van idled with its lights off and the side door already open. Dara put Soto inside first, then climbed in after him.

"Go," she said to the driver, and the van moved.

After

They were three kilometres away and Dara was pressing a thermal pad against the cut on Soto's forearm — a souvenir of the maintenance shaft — when he finally asked what she assumed he had been building toward since the server room.

"Who sent you?"

"People who need what you know."

"The trial data." He said it the way you say the name of a disease. "They want to use it."

"They want to publish it."

He was quiet for a moment. The van moved through city lights that strobed across his face in the darkness, painting him in intervals. Outside, the Vanek Tower would be locked down now — silent and sealed and missing exactly one person.

"Will it be enough?" he asked. "Publishing it. Will it change anything?"

Dara pressed the pad against his arm gently and said nothing, because she had learned that the specifics follow you home, and she had enough of her own. But she thought — privately, quietly — that it had better be. Because the next building would be taller, the next clock would run shorter, and even she had only so many near-misses left before one of them stopped being near.

The city slid past the dark windows. Dara Voss cleaned her hands and watched it go.
