Cataract Run

"Sometimes rivers go down."

✍️ Jerome Geraldo
~1,550 words
⏱ ~8 min read

Rafi Santos never liked boats. Boats promised rules—weight limits, balance, patience. Rafi preferred things you could run from. But when the jungle ate the last mile of road and the sun sank behind a wall of green, the river was the only way to reach San Orencio before the fever did.

The vial in his pocket was no bigger than his thumb, wrapped in cloth and sealed in a plastic tube. Antivenom, field-grade, stolen off a cartel plane that had tried to land on a strip made of mud and bravado. Rafi didn’t steal for profit anymore; he stole for debts, and the biggest debt had a small face and a weak cough.

“Your cousin’s kid?” Captain Inez asked, squinting at him across the dock. Her boat wasn’t really a boat so much as a long, narrow argument with the water—an outboard motor bolted to a canoe reinforced with scavenged metal.

“My sister’s,” Rafi said.

Inez’s gaze flicked to his pocket. “That?”

He nodded.

“You got people behind you?”

Rafi listened. Somewhere upriver, a howler monkey screamed like a siren. “Yes.”

Inez spat into the river. “Then we leave now.”

They pushed off just as the first engine noise rolled through the trees—a low growl, hungry and certain. The river accepted them with a slap, dark water licking the hull. Inez steered by instinct and scars, cutting through channels that looked identical to Rafi: black water, hanging vines, the occasional pale log that might not be a log.

Rafi sat low, one hand on the gun he’d sworn he’d stop carrying. He’d meant it, too. But promises didn’t hold much weight out here.

A light appeared behind them, bouncing through the jungle—headlamps. The cartel’s boats. Fast ones.

Inez muttered, “They brought the greyhounds.”

Rafi’s throat tightened. “Can we outrun them?”

“We can out-know them.” She jerked her chin. “Hold on.”

The river narrowed into a throat of rock. Current accelerated, pulling them forward. Inez angled the canoe toward a gap between boulders, so tight Rafi could have reached out and touched both sides. The boat shot through, scraping metal, sparks hissing into water.

Behind them, a pursuing boat tried the same line and misjudged. The crunch echoed off stone. Men cursed. A rifle cracked, the shot whining over Rafi’s head and punching a hole in a leaf the size of a dinner plate.

Rafi fired once into the darkness—not to hit, just to speak in the language they understood. The jungle swallowed the sound.

“Don’t waste rounds,” Inez snapped. “Save them for the falls.”

“What falls?”

Instead of answering, she gunned the motor. The canoe surged forward, and the river began to change—its surface roughening, voice rising into a constant roar. Mist cooled Rafi’s face. The jungle opened enough for moonlight to show white water ahead, foaming like teeth.

“You said this was a river route,” Rafi shouted over the noise.

“It is,” Inez shouted back. “Sometimes rivers go down.”

The cataract appeared suddenly—water dropping into darkness, a curtain of spray and violence. Rafi’s stomach tried to climb out of his body.

Inez aimed for the left side where the fall broke into steps, smaller drops like a staircase built by a reckless god. “If we take it straight, we die. If we take it slow, they catch us.”

“And if we take it your way?”

Inez grinned, fierce. “We might live.”

The cartel boats burst into view behind them—two, then three—cutting the water with bright headlamps. Gunfire stitched the mist. One round shattered the metal rail beside Rafi’s knee.

He grabbed the antivenom tube, shoved it deeper into his shirt, and braced.

The canoe hit the first drop. For a heartbeat they were weightless, hanging in spray. Then the hull slammed down, water exploding over them. The motor screamed, kept turning. Inez corrected hard, steering into the second drop.

The cartel boats hesitated at the lip. Their lights danced like nervous eyes. One tried anyway—too fast, too confident—caught sideways on the first step and flipped. Men tumbled into foam.

Rafi watched a hand vanish under the water. The river didn’t care who you were.

Their canoe bucked down the third step, nearly throwing Rafi. He grabbed the gunwale with both hands. The antivenom tube pressed into his ribs like a reminder: don’t die, don’t die, don’t die.

At the fourth step, a rock jutted up, hidden by churn. Inez saw it too late. The hull struck with a metallic shriek. The canoe spun, half-submerged, and the motor coughed.

Inez slapped the throttle. Nothing. The motor died in a wet, final sputter.

The current grabbed them and dragged them toward the last drop—bigger than the others, a real fall this time. No motor meant no steering. Just gravity and whatever mercy the river had left.

Rafi tore off his shirt, wrapped it around his hand, and reached into the exposed motor housing. Water streamed over his wrist. He felt for the fuel line, found it loose—shaken free by the impact. He jammed it back on, fingers numb.

Behind them, one cartel boat dared the steps, slower now, careful. Its headlamp beam swept over Rafi and Inez like a spotlight in a courtroom.

“Rafi!” Inez yelled. “Now!”

He yanked the starter cord. The motor coughed once.

The canoe tipped over the lip of the final fall.

For an endless second, they dropped through mist, river roaring in Rafi’s skull. He pulled the cord again midair, absurdly.

The motor caught—miraculously—right as they hit the plunge pool below. The canoe slammed down, but this time it moved, engine biting water, Inez wrenching the tiller to keep them upright.

They shot into calmer current, dripping and alive.

The cartel boat appeared at the top of the fall, its light glaring down. It didn’t follow. Either the captain had learned fear, or he’d run out of men willing to drown for pride.

Inez laughed, breathless. Rafi couldn’t.

Hours later, the jungle thinned into scattered huts and a dock lit by a single bulb. San Orencio smelled like woodsmoke and sickness. A woman waited with a child wrapped in blankets, his lips pale, his eyes glassy.

Rafi stumbled onto the dock, hands shaking as he pulled out the tube. “Here,” he said, voice breaking on the word.

His sister took it like it was holy. She didn’t ask what he’d done to get it. She didn’t have to.

As dawn bled into the sky, Rafi sat on the dock beside Inez’s battered canoe. The river flowed on, innocent again, as if it hadn’t tried to kill them all night.

Inez lit a cigarette and offered him one. “Still don’t like boats?” she asked.

Rafi watched his sister kneel with the medic, watched the child’s chest rise and fall, watched the jungle keep its distance for once.

“I like this one,” he said. “Just don’t tell it.”
