Dust and Due

"Every town has one man the law can't touch. Marshal Cole makes it her business to disagree."

✍️ Jerome Geraldo
~1,480 words
⏱ ~7 min read

Calloway had been a mining town once, before the vein ran out and the company pulled its workers south in six wagons on a Thursday afternoon in September, leaving behind a main street of twelve buildings, a church with a broken bell, and forty-two people who either could not leave or had decided not to. Marshal Elsa Cole knew all of this from the surveyor's report she had read on the overnight coach from Millhaven, because she was methodical in the way of someone who has made a career long enough to understand that the difference between arriving alive and arriving otherwise is usually preparation.

She had been sent to collect a debt — specifically, to retrieve Jerome Hatch, formerly of the Millhaven Territorial Guarantee Company, who had absconded with eighteen thousand dollars of client funds and was, according to three separate informants, living in Calloway under the name Pearce. The warrant was valid. The bounty was fair. The job was, by her standards, routine.

She encountered the first problem before she had cleared Calloway's western road: a boy, perhaps twelve, who was standing in the middle of the dust track with his hat off and his hands visible in the way that someone had clearly told him to display them, looking at her horse rather than at her.

"You'll want to turn around," he said, with the flat affect of someone delivering a message learned by rote. "Mr. Crane says the town is closed."

Mr. Crane

"Mr. Crane," said Elsa, "is not a municipal authority." She rode past the boy at a walk. He did not move to stop her, which suggested his instructions had extended only to the message and not to enforcement of it. She filed this under useful.

Calloway was still in the way that towns with people hiding in them are still. The main street showed signs of habitation — a chicken loose near the livery, smoke from two chimneys, a curtain that moved in the general store window as she passed — but no one in the street itself. The saloon at the east end of the main road had its doors wedged open and a man sitting outside on a chair tilted back against the wall, hat low, rifle across his knees, watching her approach with the patience of someone who has been sitting in that chair for eleven days and has resolved to sit in it eleven more if that is what is required.

He was perhaps sixty, with the specific kind of stillness that came not from ease but from long discipline over impulse. He wore no badge. He wore no expression she could read from thirty feet, then twenty, then ten as she rode up and stopped her horse and looked down at him.

"Marshal," he said. Not a greeting. An acknowledgement.

"You're Crane," she said.

"That's what they call me."

"I'm told you've been holding this town."

He considered this. "Holding is a strong word. The people here have agreed to go nowhere and say nothing until I am satisfied with what I came here for."

"Agreed under duress," she said, "is not agreed."

"Every agreement is under some duress," he said. "The question is whether the duress is proportionate to the thing being agreed to. I think mine is."

The Dead Man's Position

He told her what he had come for with the same flat patience he had used for everything else. His name, he said, was not Crane. His name was Amos Whitfield, and he had been a partner in the Millhaven Territorial Guarantee Company until three years ago, when Jerome Hatch had filed false documentation certifying Whitfield's death, collected the death benefit from the company insurer, dissolved the partnership with Whitfield's notarised signature — forged — and disappeared with the company's liquid assets.

"You have a death certificate in my name," he said. "Filed, certified, accepted by the court. In the eyes of the law, I cannot be a claimant, because I do not exist. In the eyes of the law, I cannot testify, because my testimony is the testimony of a ghost. In the eyes of the law, Jerome Hatch did nothing wrong, because the records say I consented to the dissolution. The only thing the law has not yet been presented with is me, here, alive, in the same town as the man who filed those papers."

Elsa looked at him. "So you've been waiting."

"He's here. In the building behind me." He tilted his head toward the saloon. "Has been since I arrived. He knows if he steps out, I'll take him to the circuit court myself. He knows he can't stay in there forever. He is waiting for me to run out of patience." A pause. "He has miscalculated."

Elsa considered the situation. She had a warrant for Jerome Hatch on the theft charge. Technically she was obliged to execute it, take Hatch to Millhaven, and let the courts address the rest. Technically Whitfield was obstructing, and she could compel him to stand aside.

She was thinking about the word technically.

Resolution, Millhaven-Style

She had been a marshal for eleven years. In that time she had executed warrants she agreed with and warrants she didn't. She had delivered men she liked to courts that sentenced them unjustly, and men she despised to acquittals she found infuriating. The law, she had concluded, was a tool that cut in both directions, and the quality of what it built depended entirely on who was holding it and what they were making.

"Get Hatch out of the saloon," she said.

Whitfield looked at her. "I am not sure you have the authority—"

"I have the warrant," she said. "Which means I have the authority to place him in my custody, which I am doing. You are a witness. In my custody there is no dispute about your legal existence, because I am looking at you and I am not a court. I am taking Hatch to Millhaven. You will ride with us. When we arrive, you will present yourself to the circuit court in person, and Hatch will be there when you do, and a living man's testimony given in the physical presence of the man who certified him dead is going to be a great deal more compelling than paperwork."

There was a silence. Whitfield studied her with the same patient, unreadable expression.

"You have done this sort of thing before," he said.

"Not this exactly," she said. "But close."

He stood from the chair, slow and careful in the way of a man who has been sitting in a tilted chair for eleven days. He called Hatch's name into the saloon. After a long pause — the pause of a man calculating odds and finding them all poor — Hatch came out. He was smaller than Elsa had expected, with the diminished look of someone who has been afraid for eleven days straight and has run out of the energy pretending otherwise requires.

They rode out of Calloway at midmorning, three of them on the road south to Millhaven, with the dust rising behind them in the still air. The forty-two residents of Calloway came out to watch from their doorways and windows — not cheering, not anything as dramatic as that, simply watching in the way of people who have been held still for a long time and are now permitted to move again.

Elsa did not look back. She never looked back on a job. Three days from now she would file her report and collect her fee and take the next assignment from the board. That was the nature of the work. The road was always there. You rode it until you didn't.
