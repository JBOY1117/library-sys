Six Stops to Mercy

"Tasks are proof."

✍️ Jerome Geraldo
~1,450 words
⏱ ~7 min read
💌

The first time Nora Hale saw him, he was arguing gently with a broken ticket machine like the machine might be persuaded by politeness. He wore a rain-dark jacket and carried a paper bag folded tight at the top. When the machine refused him again, he sighed—not angry, just tired—and turned as if to leave.

Nora, standing behind him with her own ticket in hand, said, “It only takes coins on Tuesdays.”

He blinked. “That’s not a real rule.”

“It is here,” she said. “This station runs on folklore.”

He laughed, surprised into it, and something in Nora loosened—some knot she’d been keeping tight since she’d moved back home to take care of her grandmother and discovered that the town hadn’t saved her a place so much as a shadow.

“Do you have coins?” he asked.

Nora dug into her pocket, came up with a few quarters. “If you promise not to fight it again.”

“I promise,” he said solemnly, as if swearing an oath to steel and circuitry.

He fed the machine, got his ticket, and finally looked at her properly. “I’m Eli,” he said.

“Nora.”

Their train arrived with a squeal that sounded like a complaint. Inside, the seats were half empty. Eli chose one across the aisle from Nora—not too close, not too far—and set his paper bag on his lap like it mattered.

Nora tried not to stare at the bag. She failed.

“What’s in there?” she asked, then regretted it immediately.

Eli didn’t seem offended. He hesitated, then opened the top just enough to show a corner of something soft and pink. “Donuts,” he said. “For Mercy.”

Nora’s heart did a small, foolish stumble. “Your daughter?”

“My dog,” he said, and his smile this time was less surprised and more… private. “She’s old. She can’t smell well anymore. But she still remembers donuts.”

“Mercy,” Nora repeated, tasting the name. “That’s sweet.”

“It’s what she taught me,” he said, and then the train lurched forward and whatever else he might have said got folded into motion.

Over the next week, Nora saw Eli again at the same station, same hour. He struggled with the ticket machine in a new way—today it demanded exact change, tomorrow it declared “OUT OF SERVICE” while still taking money like a thief.

Nora began carrying coins on purpose.

They started sitting together, first across the aisle, then side by side, their shoulders sometimes touching when the train rounded the bend by the river. Eli always carried the bag. Nora always asked about Mercy.

“She sleeps more now,” Eli said one morning, voice careful. “I carry her outside so she can feel the sun.”

Nora nodded, understanding more than she should. Her grandmother, June, slept in the afternoons and woke confused, searching the house for rooms that no longer existed in her mind.

“Do you ever feel like you’re becoming a translator,” Nora said, “for someone who’s halfway in another language?”

Eli looked at her with a seriousness that warmed and scared her. “Yes,” he said. “Exactly.”

On a Thursday, the train stopped unexpectedly between stations. An announcement crackled: “Mechanical delay. Ten minutes.” Ten became twenty. Rain tapped the windows with increasing impatience.

Eli shifted, restless. “I have to get back,” he said. “Mercy needs her meds at noon.”

Nora checked her phone. No signal. The town’s old dead zones didn’t care about modern schedules.

“Can I help?” Nora asked. “I have a car at the next stop.”

Eli hesitated, pride warring with urgency. Then he nodded. “If we can even get moving.”

Nora stood, walked to the conductor, asked questions in the firm voice she used with doctors. When she returned, she said, “They’re going to tow us to Crestview Station. Fifteen minutes.”

Eli exhaled like he’d been holding breath since last year. “Thank you,” he said, and the words carried more weight than the situation alone deserved.

At Crestview, they hurried through puddles to Nora’s old sedan. Eli sat carefully, the donut bag tucked close. Nora drove faster than she usually dared on wet roads, following Eli’s directions into a neighborhood of small houses and tired gardens.

When they arrived, Mercy was waiting on the porch—an old golden retriever with clouded eyes and a tail that wagged on faith rather than sight.

Eli knelt, stroking her face. “I’m here,” he whispered. “I’m here.”

Nora stood back, suddenly feeling like she’d stepped into a sacred room. Mercy sniffed the air, turned her head toward Nora, and wuffed once as if approving.

Eli looked up. “Do you want to come in?” he asked. “You can meet her properly.”

Inside, Eli’s house smelled like coffee and dog shampoo and something baked long ago into the walls. He gave Mercy her medicine with practiced gentleness. Then he opened the donut bag and broke a piece for her. Mercy chewed slowly, eyes half closed in bliss.

Nora found herself smiling, then swallowing hard.

Eli watched her. “Who are you taking care of?” he asked quietly.

Nora’s shoulders lifted, fell. “My grandmother,” she admitted. “She raised me. Now she forgets my name sometimes. I pretend it doesn’t hurt. I’m not good at pretending.”

Eli nodded like he’d been given a true thing and would treat it carefully. “I’m sorry,” he said.

Nora looked at Mercy, at the slow rhythm of her breathing. “It’s strange,” she said. “How love turns into tasks.”

“It turns into proof,” Eli corrected softly. “Tasks are proof.”

They sat on the couch while Mercy slept against Eli’s feet. The rain eased. The room filled with the small noises of an ordinary life holding itself together.

When Nora finally stood to leave, Eli walked her to the door. On the porch, he held the empty donut bag like a token.

“I owe you,” he said.

Nora shook her head. “You don’t.”

“I do,” he insisted, then paused, searching for the right shape of honesty. “Not for the ride. For… making it feel less lonely.”

Nora felt the words land in her chest, gentle but undeniable. “I can’t promise I’ll fix anything,” she said.

Eli’s smile was sad and steady. “I don’t need fixing,” he said. “I need company.”

Nora surprised herself by reaching for his hand. His fingers were warm, slightly rough, the hand of someone who carried weight without complaint.

“Tomorrow,” she said, “the ticket machine will demand goats or poetry or whatever it does.”

Eli squeezed her hand once. “Then I’ll bring extra coins,” he said. “And if you want… you can come meet Mercy again.”

Nora nodded, feeling the world shift by a fraction—no grand rescue, no miracle cure, just a new stop added to the route.

“Tomorrow,” she agreed.

And for the first time in a long while, the word sounded like something she could hold.
