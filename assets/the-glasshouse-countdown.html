The Glasshouse Countdown

"The countdown wasn’t to forgetting."

✍️ Jerome Geraldo
~1,650 words
⏱ ~8 min read
🔪

Mina Kestrel learned the truth about panic the day she realized her front door had been unlocked for three hours.

Not open. Not broken. Simply unlocked—quietly, politely—as if whoever came in wanted her to understand that locks were suggestions.

She stood in her kitchen staring at the brass bolt, hand hovering inches from it. The apartment around her looked normal: sink full of dishes, work laptop on the table, the small plant she kept alive out of spite.

Then she saw the note.

It was placed dead center on her table, aligned with the wood grain like a designer had laid it out. Her name was written on the outside in black ink: MINA.

She didn’t touch it at first. She checked the rooms. Closet. Bathroom. Under the bed—ridiculous, but fear liked rituals. She listened for breathing that wasn’t hers. Nothing.

Only when she’d convinced herself there wasn’t a person inside her home did she return to the table and open the note.

You have 24 hours to remember what you saw at Glasshouse.
If you don’t, we will help you forget everything else.

Mina read it twice, then a third time like repetition could change meaning.

Glasshouse wasn’t a place she spoke about. It was an office building downtown with mirrored windows and a lobby that smelled like money. Two years ago, she’d been a junior data auditor there—nobody important, nobody worth threatening. Then she’d found irregularities in a charitable fund, patterns that looked like mistakes until they looked like laundering.

She’d reported it. Her manager had smiled warmly and told her she was “sharp.” That night, her car brakes had failed on the freeway.

After that, she’d learned a different kind of math: the math of survival. She’d moved apartments, changed routines, stopped taking calls from unknown numbers. She’d told herself the threat had passed.

Now, with the note on her table, she understood it hadn’t passed. It had waited.

Her phone buzzed. Unknown number.

Mina answered without thinking, because fear sometimes grabbed the wheel.

A man’s voice, calm and pleasant, said, “Mina Kestrel. Thank you for picking up.”

“Who is this?” she demanded, hating the thin edge in her own voice.

“Someone with an interest in your memory,” he said. “Do you recall what you saw at Glasshouse on the evening of March 14th?”

Mina’s stomach dropped. She did recall—flashes. A server room door propped open. A man she didn’t recognize using her boss’s badge. A file folder stamped with a logo she’d never seen again. And a woman—smiling—who’d turned and looked straight at Mina as if Mina had been expected.

“I don’t know what you’re talking about,” Mina lied.

The man sighed, like a teacher disappointed by a predictable student. “We are not negotiating facts. We are negotiating outcomes.”

Mina’s mind raced. “If you can break into my apartment, why leave a note? Why call?”

“Because we don’t want you dead,” he said, almost kindly. “Dead people create investigations. We want you quiet. Quiet is easier when it’s voluntary.”

Mina’s fingers clenched around the phone. “What do you want?”

“To see whether you still have the file,” he said.

Her blood went cold. The file—her insurance policy. After the brake incident, she’d broken into her own work archive and copied everything onto a drive she hid in a place no one would think to look. She hadn’t opened it in a year. She’d been too afraid of what it might cost to use it.

“I don’t have anything,” she said.

“Then you have 24 hours to remember,” the man replied, as if that made sense. “At the end of that time, we will come again. This time we will not bother with paper.”

The call ended.

Mina stood shaking, then forced herself to move. Motion was the only thing that turned terror into strategy.

She grabbed her laptop, opened an encrypted folder, and stared at a blank password field. The password was a sentence she’d once known by muscle memory. Now her mind offered nothing but fog.

The note’s wording returned: remember what you saw.

Not “remember what you saved.” Not “hand it over.” Remember.

A sick thought formed: they didn’t know whether she still had the file. They wanted to test her—and possibly trigger something. Maybe her memory had gaps because of trauma. Or because someone had made sure it did.

Mina grabbed her coat and went to the only person she trusted: Jonas Reeve, a former investigative reporter who now did “risk consulting” for people who couldn’t afford official help. He lived above a print shop and always smelled faintly of ink.

Jonas opened the door, took one look at her face, and stepped aside. “Inside,” he said.

Mina handed him the note. He read it once, then again, eyes narrowing.

“Glasshouse,” he said. “That name circles back every few years. People who ask questions stop asking.”

“They want me to remember,” Mina said. “I think I’m missing something. I can’t even unlock my own archive.”

Jonas sat, already building a map in his head. “Tell me everything you can remember.”

Mina closed her eyes and forced herself back into that night: the hum of the server room, fluorescent lights, her boss’s perfume. The smiling woman. The folder. The logo.

“What did it look like?” Jonas asked.

Mina frowned, concentrating until her temples hurt. “A circle,” she said slowly. “But not closed. Like… a ring with a bite taken out.”

Jonas’s expression changed—recognition, then alarm. “That’s not just a company mark,” he said. “That’s a private security syndicate. They don’t sue people. They erase them.”

Mina’s mouth went dry. “How do I un-erase myself?”

Jonas stood and pulled a small lockbox from a shelf. “First,” he said, “we assume your apartment is compromised. Phones, laptop, everything. Second, we move your data—if you still have it. Third, we get you to someone who can grant protection.”

Mina’s throat tightened. “Police?”

Jonas hesitated. “Eventually. Maybe. But if Glasshouse has friends inside the system, police becomes a coin toss.”

He handed her a burner phone. “No names. No contacts. Only calls you make in front of me.”

Mina took it. “What about the 24 hours?”

Jonas’s eyes were grim. “We use them.”

They drove to Mina’s building in Jonas’s beat-up car, parking a block away. Jonas watched the windows while Mina went in alone, heart pounding like it wanted out of her ribs.

Her apartment door was closed. Locked, this time. That didn’t comfort her. It meant someone had learned she noticed.

Inside, nothing looked disturbed. Too neat. Mina went straight to the place she’d hidden the drive: taped beneath the underside of a drawer in her dresser, behind a false strip of wood.

The tape was cut cleanly.

The drive was gone.

Mina stared, numb. Her insurance policy had been removed like a tooth.

On her table sat another note, perfectly aligned.

12 hours.

Mina’s hands shook as she picked it up. Beneath it was a small object: a USB drive identical to hers, placed like a gift.

Jonas, waiting outside, would tell her not to touch it. But Jonas wasn’t in the room, and Mina understood the cruelty of the setup: they were giving her a choice between ignorance and infection.

She didn’t plug it in. She put it in a plastic bag, grabbed her coat, and left without looking around again. Some part of her was sure that if she looked, she’d see someone sitting in her chair smiling politely.

Downstairs, Jonas saw her face and knew. “They took it,” he said.

“And left this,” Mina replied, showing him the bag.

Jonas’s jaw tightened. “A trojan,” he said. “Or a message.”

Mina stared at the building’s mirrored lobby doors reflecting streetlight. Glasshouse. Mirrors. Reflections. Memory.

“They don’t want the file,” Mina said suddenly. The idea hit her with sick clarity. “They want me to open it. They want me to do it for them, on a system they can track.”

Jonas nodded slowly. “Which means the file you stole wasn’t the whole story,” he said. “It was the bait. The real story is in your head.”

Mina’s pulse thundered. “Then how do I remember?”

Jonas looked at her like he hated the answer. “We force the memory back,” he said. “Sometimes trauma blocks. Sometimes… chemicals do. I know a doctor who used to treat whistleblowers.”

Mina swallowed fear like bitter medicine. “Do it.”

They drove across town to a discreet clinic with no sign on the door. Inside, a tired-eyed doctor asked Mina questions, shone light into her eyes, ran simple tests.

Then the doctor said softly, “Your hippocampal recall looks disrupted. That can happen naturally. It can also happen with targeted sedatives.”

Mina’s hands curled into fists. “They drugged me.”

“Likely,” the doctor said. “I can’t restore what isn’t there. But I can help your brain stop avoiding it.”

An hour later, Mina lay on a narrow bed with electrodes on her temples, guided through breathing, through narrative reconstruction. Jonas sat in the corner, silent.

Mina spoke haltingly at first, then faster as images sharpened: the server room. The folder. The smiling woman stepping closer.

And then—finally—the missing piece clicked into place: the woman had said something, a sentence that had sounded meaningless at the time.

Mina’s eyes snapped open. “She said,” Mina whispered, “‘If you ever forget, check the aquarium.’”

Jonas leaned forward. “Aquarium where?”

Mina sat up, heart racing. “Glasshouse lobby,” she said. “They had a huge wall aquarium. Everyone loved it. I used to sit there at lunch.”

Jonas stood. “Then we go now,” he said.

Mina shook her head. “They’ll be waiting.”

Jonas’s gaze was steady. “They already are,” he said. “But if the clue is real, it’s the first advantage you’ve had in two years.”

They arrived at Glasshouse just before midnight. The lobby was empty except for a security guard half-asleep at his desk. The aquarium wall glowed blue-green, fish drifting like slow thoughts.

Mina approached the glass. Her reflection stared back—pale, determined, terrified. She scanned the base of the tank, the decorative stones, the artificial coral.

Then she saw it: a maintenance panel at the bottom edge, slightly misaligned. A seam.

Jonas produced a small multitool. “Tell me you used to watch too many spy movies,” Mina whispered.

“I used to be a reporter,” he said, and pried.

The panel popped free. Inside was a waterproof envelope taped to the frame. Mina’s hands shook as she pulled it out. Her name was written on it—in the same neat black ink as the notes.

Inside was a microSD card and a handwritten line:

You remembered. Now run.

Behind them, the security guard’s chair creaked.

A calm voice spoke from the lobby’s shadow. “Mina Kestrel,” it said pleasantly. “Thank you for coming back to Glasshouse.”

Mina turned and saw the smiling woman from her memory—older by two years, same eyes. Beside her stood silhouettes in suits, too still to be ordinary.

Jonas grabbed Mina’s wrist. “Move,” he hissed.

Mina’s fear became motion. They sprinted for the exit, the aquarium’s blue light chasing them like water. Behind them, footsteps followed—measured, unhurried, confident.

Outside, the night air hit Mina like freedom and threat at once. Jonas shoved her toward the car.

Mina clutched the envelope, mind blazing with one clear thought amid the panic:

They hadn’t wanted her dead.

They’d wanted her back in the maze.

And now that she’d found the hidden piece, the countdown wasn’t to her forgetting.

It was to them making sure she didn’t get to remember anything ever again.
