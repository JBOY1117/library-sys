The Last Candle

"Some grief goes so deep it burns a hole through the world."

✍️ Jerome Geraldo
~1,450 words
⏱ ~7 min read

Mara lit the first candle the night her mother died. A small white stub pressed into the neck of a wine bottle, its flame no taller than her thumbnail. She watched it burn until it guttered out and she thought: there. That is her now. A little light, then nothing.

The second candle came when her brother Victor drowned in the quarry lake the following spring. She bought a proper candle that time — cream-coloured beeswax from the church gift shop — and set it in the window of the bedroom he had shared with her growing up. She sat on his mattress, still pressed flat by the shape of him, and she whispered his name until the wick drooped over and the wax pooled on the sill like a wound.

Mara was not a superstitious woman. She was a primary school teacher who graded papers on Sunday afternoons and kept a lemon plant on her kitchen counter. She recycled. She voted. She believed in antibiotics and grief therapy and the slow, unglamorous work of surviving loss. The candles were only ritual. A way of marking, not of calling.

She told herself this every time.

The Ritual Grows

Her father followed Victor in the autumn — a stroke, quick and indifferent as a closing door. Candle three. Then came her colleague Ruth, killed in a motorway collision on a Thursday morning she should have been marking Year Four essays. Candle four. Mara's childhood friend Saoirse lost a long, ugly fight with her lungs that winter. Candle five. And then, in a year that seemed to have been designed specifically to hollow Mara out, her husband Daniel walked into the sea off the coast of Galway and did not walk back. The coastguard found his shoes on the rocks. Nothing else.

Candle six burned for three days. Mara sat before it and did not eat, did not sleep. She watched the flame convulse in the draught from the kitchen and she thought she saw it lean — actually lean — toward her, as if it recognised her. As if it had been waiting.

"The dead don't go," her grandmother had told her once, pressing dry fingers against the child-Mara's cheek. "They go still. There's a difference."

She had never understood that distinction until now.

The Seventh Night

It was the anniversary of her mother's death — seven years to the night — when Mara understood that she was out of ordinary grief. Not through any mystical revelation, but through the simple horror of arithmetic. Six people. Seven years. One woman left behind. She sat at the kitchen table with the lemon plant dying in its pot and the November rain nailing itself to the windows, and she felt the house breathe.

Houses do not breathe.

She lit the seventh candle from the last match in the box. The flame caught immediately, so fast it seemed like the fire had already been there, waiting in the wick all along. It was an old candle she found in the back of the airing cupboard — she had no memory of buying it, no memory of it being in the house at all. Black wax. The smell of it was wrong: not smoke, not vanilla, not any candle smell she could name. It was the smell of a room not opened in a long time.

She set it on the table and spoke six names.

The flame did not move. The house was perfectly still. Outside, the rain had stopped without her noticing. She sat in the silence and waited for the nothing that always followed — the flat, grey nothing that grief left behind when the ritual was done.

Instead, from the hallway, she heard footsteps.

What Comes When You Call

They were not dramatic footsteps. Not the lurching, deliberate horror-film tread she might have imagined. They were ordinary footsteps, the small shuffling steps of someone who knew the layout of the house in the dark and was moving carefully through it, half-awake, the way people do when they get up for water at two in the morning. The way Daniel used to.

She did not move. Her hands lay flat on the table on either side of the candle. The flame still did not flicker.

The footsteps paused outside the kitchen door.

"Daniel?" she said. Her voice came out lower than she expected. Levelled down by the weight of certainty she should not have had. She believed. She believed before she saw anything, and that was the thing that stayed with her afterward — not the horror of what happened, but the horror of how much a part of her had wanted it.

The door opened.

The figure in the doorway had Daniel's height and Daniel's way of standing — slightly forward, as if perpetually ready to greet someone. But the light from the candle, thin as it was, revealed the colour wrong in all of it. Skin the shade of old paper. Eyes open too wide, the pupils dilated to black discs that swallowed the candlelight rather than reflected it. His mouth carried a smile she had never seen on his face in life — not Daniel's warm, off-centre grin, but something pulled geometric, something that did not originate in feeling.

"Mara," it said, in Daniel's voice, and the sound of her name in that voice — familiar and utterly wrong at the same moment — broke something in her chest that she did not know she had been protecting.

She did not run. Later, she could not explain why. Perhaps because her legs would not take the instruction. Perhaps because part of her was still the woman who had sat over six candles, calling and calling, and had not really expected an answer, and was now confronted with the responsibility of having received one.

She blew out the candle.

What Darkness Teaches

In the sudden, total dark, there was nothing. No breathing. No presence. The house was just a house again — cold and ordinary and smelling of rain and the lemon plant on the counter. She sat alone for a long time with her hands pressed against her eyes, and when she finally opened them the kitchen was empty and the seventh candle had burned down to nothing, leaving a black stain on the tabletop like a signature.

She never lit another candle.

She began, slowly, to leave lights on. First the kitchen light, then the hallway, then the small lamp on the landing outside the bedroom. Not because she was afraid of the dark. She was a rational woman who believed in antibiotics and grief therapy and the slow work of surviving. She left the lights on because she understood now what her grandmother had meant all those years ago, pressing dry fingers to a child's face.

The dead go still. They do not go away.

And some griefs, Mara had learned, are not a flame burning down. Some griefs are the candle itself — the substance, the wick, the waxy dark matter of what you are — and what you risk, when you let that darkness speak, is not being haunted but being summoned.

Being the one who opens the door.
