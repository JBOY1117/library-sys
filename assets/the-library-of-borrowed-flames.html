The Library of Borrowed Flames

"Mercy is expensive."

✍️ Jerome Geraldo
~1,500 words
⏱ ~8 min read

In Arclight City, fire was a regulated substance.

Not the common kind—kitchen flame, candle wick, forge heat. Those were ordinary, messy, allowed. The regulated fire was the old kind: living flame that remembered names, flames that could speak, flames that could heal or curse depending on the story you fed them. Those fires belonged to the Crown, which meant they belonged to the Vault, which meant they belonged—practically—to the Great Library, where the Vault was buried like a secret under knowledge.

Tamsin Vale was a junior librarian, which in Arclight meant she had the privilege of dusting histories nobody read and the honor of never touching anything important.

She intended to touch something important anyway.

It began with a patron request: a miner from the ash district, hands cracked and eyes red, asking for records about “lungstone,” a sickness that turned breath into grit. He said the healers’ tinctures weren’t working. He said he’d heard, from someone who’d heard, that the old fires could burn sickness out like rot from wood.

Tamsin knew the rule: No one seeks the Vault for personal need. Personal need led to personal bargains, and bargains were how kingdoms ended up ruled by creatures that smiled too widely.

Still, when the miner slid a folded cloth across the desk—a child’s scarf, tiny, grey with soot—Tamsin felt something in her choose a side.

That night, after the Library bells marked closing, she stayed behind under the pretense of reshelving. When the senior archivists finally left, keys clinking like warnings, Tamsin slipped into the Restricted Stacks.

The air changed there. Books whispered—not aloud, but in the way paper can sound like breath when it’s old enough. Lantern light bent strangely around certain shelves, avoiding titles that didn’t want to be read.

She found the stairwell to the lower levels and descended until her lantern’s flame turned blue.

At the bottom was a door of black iron engraved with a motto: KNOWLEDGE IS KINDLING.

Tamsin had no key. But she had something better: the catalog.

Librarians in Arclight memorized the catalog the way priests memorized prayers. Not the public index—the true one, the hidden lattice of cross-references that linked book to book, secret to secret. The Vault’s lock was built on that logic: it opened only to someone who could name, precisely, what lay behind it.

Tamsin placed her palm on the iron and spoke, softly, as if addressing a shy animal.

“Open to the shelf where the Crown stored the flame taken from the throat of the first dragon that died of loneliness,” she said. “Cross-referenced under grief, under weather, under the metallurgy of vows.”

The iron shivered. Lines of heat traced the engraving. The door swung inward on silent hinges.

Inside, the Vault was not full of gold. It was full of jars.

Hundreds of glass vessels lined the walls, each holding a different flame: green flame that hummed like bees, white flame that flickered to unheard music, black flame that drank the lantern light.

Tamsin stepped in and felt the hair on her arms rise, not from heat but from attention. The flames were awake.

A voice spoke—not from one jar, but from the room itself.

“Librarian,” it said, amused. “You are not permitted.”

Tamsin swallowed. “I’m borrowing,” she managed. “I’ll return it.”

The room’s laughter was a ripple of heat. “All borrowers say that.”

Tamsin approached a jar labeled in the old script: CINDER OF MERCY. The flame inside was small, steady, the color of late sunlight. It did not lunge at her like some others; it watched.

She lifted the jar, surprised by its weight—like holding a decision.

“Why do you choose that one?” the Vault-voice asked.

“Because it’s mercy,” Tamsin said.

“Mercy is expensive,” the voice replied. “What will you burn to pay?”

Tamsin’s mind flashed with images: the miner’s child coughing, her own mother dying years ago because the healers said, gently, that common fire couldn’t fix uncommon rot. Anger rose—clean, bright.

“I’ll burn my future,” she said, before she could stop herself.

The room went still. Even the most restless flames paused, as if listening.

Then the Vault-voice whispered, delighted, “Agreed.”

Tamsin stumbled back, jar clutched to her chest. She expected pain, or a brand, or a chain around her wrist. Instead she felt… lighter, as if a thread had been snipped inside her. Like a page torn out of a book she hadn’t read yet.

She fled up the stairs, out of Restricted Stacks, into the rainy street. The jar’s flame warmed her coat, but not enough to comfort.

The miner lived in a narrow apartment above a bakery that no longer baked. He opened the door with suspicion, then shock when he saw the jar.

“You’ll get hanged,” he whispered.

“Probably,” Tamsin said. “Where’s your daughter?”

The child lay on a cot, small chest rattling. Her eyes fluttered open, unfocused. Tamsin sat beside her and unscrewed the jar lid.

The Cinder of Mercy rose like a living ribbon, drifting toward the child’s mouth. It did not enter like smoke; it entered like a kiss—gentle, patient.

The child coughed once, hard. Grey dust spilled onto the pillow, then another cough, and more dust, until her breathing smoothed into a clean, surprised inhale. Color returned to her cheeks in a slow bloom.

The miner sobbed, silent so as not to scare her.

Tamsin watched, trembling. “It worked,” she whispered.

The flame curled back to the jar, dimmer now, as if it had given away part of itself gladly.

Outside, boots thundered on stairs. A heavy fist pounded the door.

“Crown Inspectors!” a voice barked. “Open!”

The miner’s eyes went wild. “Hide it—hide—”

Tamsin stood. Something inside her—some thread of future—should have offered an escape plan, a clever lie, a lucky coincidence. But the snipped-lightness in her chest provided nothing. She realized, with cold clarity, what she’d traded.

Her future had been the part of life where solutions appeared.

She grabbed the jar and opened the window. The alley below was narrow and wet. She could throw the flame and maybe save it—or keep it and doom the miner’s family.

Mercy, she thought, is expensive.

She pressed the jar into the miner’s hands. “Put it under the floorboards,” she said. “Don’t use it again unless it’s life or death. Promise me.”

He nodded frantically.

The pounding intensified. The door splintered.

Tamsin stepped forward into the room’s center, raising her hands. When the inspectors burst in—armored in polished leather, masks shaped like serene faces—she met them calmly, as if she’d rehearsed this moment. Perhaps she had, once, in the future she no longer owned.

“I took it,” she said. “Arrest me.”

They seized her wrists with iron cuffs that smelled faintly of ash. As they dragged her out, she looked back once and saw the child sitting up, blinking at the world as if seeing firelight for the first time.

In the street, rain cooled Tamsin’s cheeks. One inspector leaned close.

“You traded your future,” he murmured, as if tasting the idea. “Do you know what that means?”

Tamsin lifted her chin. “It means,” she said, voice steady, “that for once, someone else gets to have one.”

And in the Vault far below, the flames whispered her name like a bookmark placed carefully in a story not finished—only changed.
