The Sunday Letters

"Love is not always immediate. Sometimes it builds, letter by letter, in the space between strangers."

✍️ Jerome Geraldo
📖 ~1,430 words
⏱ ~7 min read
📅 2026
💌

The first letter arrived on a Sunday in October, slipped under the door of apartment 4B with the kind of precision that suggested intention rather than accident. Cleo almost threw it away. She had been in the building for three weeks and did not yet know any of her neighbours, and the envelope was blank on the outside — no name, no postage, only a faint pencil mark in the corner that later, in better light, she would recognise as a question mark.

Inside was a single paragraph, handwritten in navy ink on cream notepaper:

"There is a bakery on Henley Street that makes a cinnamon roll the size of a small planet. I went this morning and had two and I am not sorry. I am writing this because I wanted to tell someone and I am not yet sure who my someones are in this city. If you are the wrong person, please discard this. If you are the right person, I will be bringing one next Sunday."

Cleo read it twice. She did not know what to do with it. She put it on the kitchen counter and looked at it while she made coffee and concluded: the wrong apartment. Someone had the wrong apartment. The correct response was to slide it back under the door with a note indicating the error. She wrote the note. She did not slide it back.

On the following Sunday, a cinnamon roll appeared under the door, wrapped in paper, still warm.

The Letters Accumulate

In the year that followed, Cleo received thirty-seven letters. They were never about anything large. The second letter discussed the structural problems with the building's elevator, which had been making a sound the writer described as "philosophically unsettling." The fourth was a detailed account of a failed attempt to grow basil on a windowsill. The ninth was three sentences about a piece of music heard through a wall at two in the morning, played by someone who was clearly just learning and kept restarting the same passage, and the writer said they had found it, after the initial irritation, unexpectedly moving.

Cleo started writing back. She left her responses in the same location — the gap under the door — but her door, apartment 4B, which meant the writer was either in 4A or 4C. She had constructed a theory, based on the direction of the gap under the door and which letters arrived warm versus at room temperature, that it was 4A. She had not tested this theory. She was not sure she wanted to.

Her letters were different in register from the ones she received — more guarded at first, more precise, the letters of someone who chose words carefully because she expected to be held to them. The writer's letters were looser, more willing to trail off into uncertainty or digression. But over a year of correspondence they had found a middle ground, a shared tone that belonged to neither of them exclusively, which was how Cleo had eventually come to understand what was happening.

She was not corresponding with a stranger. She was corresponding with someone who was becoming not a stranger. These were different things, and the difference was significant.

The Decision

In the third year, something shifted. Not in the letters — the letters continued with the same warmth and the same unhurried honesty that had come to feel as natural as thinking. The shift was in Cleo.

She had told her friend Jess about the letters, once, expecting to find the situation absurd when described aloud. Instead she had found herself speaking carefully and at length about the cinnamon roll letter and the basil plant and the music through the wall, and Jess had looked at her with the expression of someone who already knows the conclusion of a story and is not going to be the one to say it.

"You like them," Jess said.

"I don't know them."

"Cleo," said Jess, with the patience of a very old friend, "you know them better than you know most people. You've just never seen their face."

That winter the letters continued. The thirty-eighth was about the first proper snowfall — how it changed the sound of the city, which the writer found disorienting in a way they admitted was probably symbolic of something. The thirty-ninth discussed a book. The fortieth was the shortest one: half a page, ending with a sentence that Cleo read six times: "I think about knocking, sometimes. I never quite manage it. I am not sure what I am waiting for."

She did not write back immediately. She sat with the letter for a week. Then she wrote back, four sentences: "I think I am also waiting. I am not sure it is the same thing I was waiting for at the beginning. I think that is all right. Sunday?"

The Knock

She had decided to stop. She had decided this on a Wednesday evening, sitting with the box of forty-one letters in her lap and recognising in herself a feeling that was no longer simply pleasant — a feeling that had become, without her entirely consenting to it, necessary. She had decided that necessary things that lived under a door were bad for a person, and that she should begin the process of moving toward a life in which her emotional necessities occupied a more accountable form.

She had written the last letter. Brief, warm, intentionally conclusive. She had not slid it under the door yet. She was going to do it Sunday morning. Then she was going to take a long walk and come back and be fine.

On Sunday morning, someone knocked on her door.

She stood in the hallway of her apartment for a moment. The knock had been tentative — the knock of someone who had been standing outside the door for some time already, working up to it. She had not been expecting this. She had not been not expecting it either. She realised she had been holding the unsent letter in her hand.

She opened the door.

The person standing in the corridor of the fourth floor of a building they had both lived in for three years was holding a cinnamon roll wrapped in paper. They looked at her with an expression she recognised, because it was the same expression she had been wearing for the past three years in the specific moments when she read their letters — the expression of someone confronting something they have thought about so many times in the abstract that meeting it in reality is slightly vertiginous.

"I brought—" they started.

"I know," she said. She stepped back from the door. "Come in."

She did not slide the last letter under the door. She kept it. Later, she added it to the box — forty-two letters in total — and kept the box on the shelf in the living room where she could see it from the kitchen, which was where she started most of her mornings now, making two cups of coffee, one for each of the people the apartment now contained.

On Sundays they went to the bakery on Henley Street. It was, as advertised, excellent.
