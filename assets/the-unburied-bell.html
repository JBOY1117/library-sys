The Unburied Bell

"Some bells are buried for a reason."

✍️ Jerome Geraldo
~1,650 words
⏱ ~8 min read
🕯️

The first time Lila Marek heard the bell, she thought it was a prank—some kid rolling an iron drum down the hill behind Saint Orin’s. The sound came at dusk, a dull clang that didn’t echo so much as press against the air. It made the windows of her rented house tremble in their frames and turned the tea in her cup into a nervous ripple.

In Gallowfield, people didn’t talk about Saint Orin’s. They used the church as a landmark—turn left at the boarded steeple—the way you might reference a burned tree. When Lila asked the hardware clerk if the bell ever rang, he stared at her as if she’d asked whether the cemetery had started taking reservations.

“That bell’s underground,” he said. “Been that way since ’33.”

Lila had come to Gallowfield to catalog county records, a neat contract with neat boxes. The job suited her: paper didn’t argue, ink didn’t bleed unless you made it. But the bell’s sound followed her through the stacks of the courthouse basement, into her sleep, into the small hesitations between thoughts.

The next evening it rang again—three slow strikes, each one slightly off from the last, like someone learning the weight of a hammer. Lila stepped outside. The streetlights buzzed. Beyond the last houses, Saint Orin’s rose against the tree line like a missing tooth. The steeple was gone; lightning had taken it years ago, the locals said, or maybe God had.

She walked there with a flashlight and her coat pulled tight. The church doors were chained, but the chain hung slack as if it had been opened and re-closed without being latched. When she touched it, the metal was warm.

Around back, the ground sloped toward the old mine access—sealed with concrete after the collapse that killed nineteen men. Someone had cleared the weeds. There was fresh dirt, a narrow trench like a grave someone had changed their mind about. And there, half-buried in the soil, was a bell.

Not the church bell—this one was smaller, hand-bell size, but heavy, with a cracked lip and a handle wrapped in something that looked like braided hair.

Lila didn’t pick it up. She couldn’t. Her fingers hovered above it, tingling.

From the church’s broken window, she heard a soft scraping sound—slow, rhythmic, like a chair dragged across floorboards in careful increments.

“Hello?” she called, and hated the way her voice came back wrong, as if the night had chewed it and returned it.

The scraping stopped.

Her flashlight beam caught a smear on the window frame: dark, old, glossy. Not paint. Not sap.

She backed away, and her heel struck something hard. Another bell. Then another, barely showing through the dirt like teeth in a jaw. The trench wasn’t a grave. It was a row.

The bell rang again—one strike—without being touched. It wasn’t loud. It was intimate, right up against the bones of her ears.

She ran.

At home, she locked the door, then checked it twice, then sat at her table with the courthouse keys spread out like charms. When the bell rang the third night, it came from inside the house—right beneath the floorboards.

Lila called Sheriff Danton, but he didn’t answer. She called the hardware clerk; the line rang until it clicked into silence. She opened her laptop, searched “Gallowfield 1933 Saint Orin bell underground,” and found nothing but a scanned obituary that mentioned the singing during the mine rescue—how the trapped men had “sung hymns until their throats gave out.”

At midnight, she pried up a floorboard in the pantry. The space beneath smelled like wet pennies and cold stone. Her flashlight revealed a crawlspace that shouldn’t have existed—her house was built on a slab, the landlord had said. Something in the dark shifted, and she saw that it wasn’t shadow.

It was hair. Braided, knotted, bundled—coiled into ropes that ran away from her like roots.

She followed them on hands and knees, dirt scraping her palms, the bell sound pulsing through the earth like a second heartbeat. The ropes of hair led to a small cavity where a bell sat upright, clean as if polished. Its handle was wrapped in a strip of cloth printed with tiny crosses.

A voice came from deeper in the crawlspace. Not words—breathing shaped like a hymn.

Then a hand touched the bell from the far side—fingers pale, nails broken, skin stained with mine-soot that never washed off. The bell rang once.

Lila jerked back and cracked her head against the beam. Stars exploded behind her eyes. In that moment of blur, she understood: the bells weren’t calling people to church. They were calling people down.

She scrambled out, shoved the floorboard back, and dragged her pantry shelves over it until the wood groaned. The bell rang again, more insistent, and the pantry door shuddered as if something leaned against it from within.

Lila fled to her car and drove to the courthouse, headlights slicing fog that hadn’t been there before. In the basement, she tore through boxes until she found the mine report. Inside was a folded letter, unfiled, unmarked, written in cramped pencil:

If they seal us in, do not let the bell be buried with us. The bell is how He finds the ones who listen.

At dawn, she returned to Saint Orin’s with a shovel, gasoline, and shaking hands. The trench behind the church was longer than she remembered, as if it had grown overnight. Bells protruded every few feet. Some were new, bright brass, still bearing store stickers.

She dug until she hit concrete—the mine seal—and struck it with the shovel’s edge. The bell rang beneath her feet, and from inside the church, the scraping started again, faster now, eager.

Lila poured gasoline into the trench. She lit a match, hesitated, and heard—very clearly—her own voice under the floorboards of her house, singing a hymn she didn’t know.

She threw the match.

Flames ran along the line of bells, and for a moment the night air filled with a chorus of clanging—dozens of strikes overlapping, a mad, joyful peal. The church windows flared orange. Something inside pounded against the boards. The sound became a scream, then a song, then only fire.

When it was over, the trench smoked and the bells sat blackened, warped, mute.

Lila drove out of Gallowfield that morning and never looked back. Yet sometimes, in the quiet between highway miles, she still heard it: a single, gentle ring, as if someone far below had learned her name and was practicing the shape of it on iron.
